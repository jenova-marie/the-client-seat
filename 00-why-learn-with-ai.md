# Part 0: Why I'm making my client learn with AI

R. built the app in Base44 over a couple of very caffeinated weekends, and honestly? It was good. People used it. Then it stopped being good in the specific way no-code tools stop being good: quietly, and all at once. A screen that had always loaded started timing out. A report R. needed every Monday hit a row limit and just ended, mid-list, with no error and no explanation. R. sent me a message at eleven at night that said "I think I've outgrown it" with a screenshot, and I could feel how much that sentence had cost.

So R. hired me to rebuild the thing properly: a real mobile app, a real backend, a real place where the data lives that doesn't vanish when a vendor changes a limit. I've done this kind of rebuild before. What I had not done before was make my very first decision about the project a decision about the client instead of the code.

Here it was: R. was not going to be a spectator.

Not because I needed the help. Because R. knows this app better than I ever will, and every hour I spent guessing at what a column meant or what a screen was for would be an hour R. could have answered in a sentence. And because the way most non-technical people get "onboarded" onto a software project is a shared folder, a weekly call, and a slowly growing feeling that everything real is happening somewhere they can't see. I didn't want that for R. I wanted R. in the room, reading the same files I read, adding the parts only R. could add.

The tool that makes that possible is AI. Not as a magic button. As a tutor.

## What this series is

This is not a coding course. You will not learn to program. You will not open a terminal, and if anyone tells you that you need to, send them to me.

It is a course in learning anything by making an AI teach it to you, and, just as important, in catching the AI when it's wrong. Those two skills go together. An AI is a wonderful tutor with one flaw: it sounds exactly as confident when it's right as when it's making things up. Learn to use the first thing and defend against the second, and you can teach yourself nearly anything. I'm going to use my own field as the example, because that's the project we were actually doing.

By the end, you'll be reading a real software project, the one your developer is building for you, and adding your own documents and data to it, with Claude doing the typing and you doing the deciding. All from a browser tab. No terminal, ever.

## The contract

Here's what I promise about every part after this one:

- **One idea per part.** If a part seems to be teaching three things, I've messed up. Tell me.
- **Every part has boxes called "paste this into Claude."** You copy the whole box, exactly as written, and paste it into Claude. You never type them by hand. Each box comes with a short note explaining why it's written the way it is, so that by the end you'll be writing your own.
- **Every part has a section called "Check it."** This is where I show you how to verify what Claude just told you. Not "trust but verify." Just verify. It gets easier every time.
- **Every part has a "Do it" section** with one concrete milestone: an account created, a file saved, a thing that exists at the end that didn't exist at the start. Click-by-click, with a prompt to paste if you get stuck, instead of screenshots that go stale.
- **Nothing in this series needs a paid plan until Part 5.** When we get there, I'll say so loudly, at the top, before you've done any work, so nobody hits a paywall halfway through a step.
- **The only thing you'll ever install is optional and comes in Part 10.** Everything before that happens in your browser.

## What you need today

A browser. An email address you actually check. A phone that can receive a text message, because Claude asks for one when you make an account.

That's it. Really.

If you're reading this because a developer sent it to you: hi. I'm glad you're here. You know things about this project that the rest of us are going to need, and I'd rather you learn how to put them where we can all see them than tell them to me on a call where I forget half.

If you're reading this because you're a developer and you want your client in the room: steal all of it. Change the names. It's yours.
